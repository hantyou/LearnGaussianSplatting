# 1. The idea, before the machinery

Suppose you have photographs of a stationary object from several known camera
poses. You want an image from a camera position that was never photographed.
This is **novel-view synthesis**. A photograph alone does not tell you how to move
the camera: you need some representation of the scene in three dimensions.

3D Gaussian splatting represents that scene as many soft, translucent, colored
ellipsoids. Each has a center, three axis lengths, an orientation, an opacity,
and a rule for its color. Project them into a camera, blend their image footprints,
compare the result with a photograph, and adjust their parameters by gradients.

The 2023 paper's contribution combines an anisotropic Gaussian representation,
adaptive changes to the number of Gaussians, and an efficient differentiable GPU
renderer. Those pieces together enabled high-quality real-time novel-view
rendering on its benchmarks. The achievement is a complete representation and
optimization/rendering system, not just the use of a Gaussian function.
[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04079).

## Follow one Gaussian

Imagine an elongated splat on the edge of a red car door:

1. Its **center** places it near the door in world coordinates.
2. Its **covariance** makes it long along the door edge and narrow across it.
3. A camera sees an **elliptical footprint**. Its size and orientation depend on
   the camera and the 3D shape, not on an independently painted 2D ellipse.
4. Its contribution is strongest at the footprint center and fades smoothly.
5. Splats in front of it reduce how much of its color reaches the pixel.
6. A pixel error sends gradients back to its color, opacity, center, and shape.

One ellipsoid cannot describe a whole car. Many small ones can approximate its
visible appearance. The ellipsoids are rendering primitives, not necessarily
physical objects, surface patches with exact boundaries, or probability beliefs.

## The connection to your Bayesian background

You can start from the familiar basis expansion

\[
f(x)=\sum_i w_i\phi_i(x),\qquad
\phi_i(x)=\exp[-\tfrac12(x-\mu_i)^T\Sigma_i^{-1}(x-\mu_i)].
\]

This gives you the right intuition for soft support and anisotropy. But rendered
color is not simply this sum. The camera changes the basis footprints, and
occlusion changes the coefficients through an ordered product:

\[
C(p)=\sum_i\underbrace{T_i(p)\alpha_i(p)}_{\text{visibility weight}}c_i.
\]

Unlike a standard linear basis regression, changing one opacity changes the
weights of everything behind it. Positions and basis shapes are learned too.

| Familiar concept | Connection | Where the analogy stops |
|---|---|---|
| Gaussian kernel / RBF | Smooth, local contribution; covariance sets shape | A splat is not a normalized PDF |
| Bayesian covariance | Same positive definite matrix algebra | Here it usually describes spatial extent, not parameter uncertainty |
| Sparse Bayesian learning | Both can allocate capacity selectively | Original densification/pruning is heuristic, not evidence maximization or ARD |
| Likelihood fitting | Image residuals define an optimization objective | Point estimates and gradients do not give a calibrated posterior |
| Sensor noise covariance | Can be used in a measurement model | It must be kept separate from splat-shape covariance |

An isotropic measurement-noise assumption can motivate squared image error.
A prior could turn that into MAP estimation. Neither automatically makes the
resulting splat covariance a posterior covariance. This distinction matters if
you later use Gaussians for radar uncertainty or occupancy.

## Where is the neural network?

In this first implementation there isn't a layered network. `GaussianScene`
inherits `nn.Module` so PyTorch can collect its `nn.Parameter` tensors. Adam
directly changes those tensors. You already know the important part from training
YOLO: construct a prediction, measure a loss, backpropagate, and take a step.
Here the prediction is an image generated by geometry and blending operations.

There are later methods that use a neural network to **predict** Gaussians from
images. That is a different training arrangement. Do not import that complexity
into your first understanding of scene-specific 3DGS.

## Compare representations

| Representation | Stored quantities | How an image is made |
|---|---|---|
| Mesh | Vertices, triangles, materials | Project triangles and shade visible surfaces |
| Point cloud | Discrete sample positions, perhaps colors | Needs a rule for coverage, holes, and visibility |
| Original NeRF | Neural function of location and viewing direction | Sample rays and integrate density/color |
| 3DGS | Explicit Gaussian parameters | Project footprints and composite in depth order |

The NeRF/3DGS comparison concerns typical original formulations. Later NeRFs
also use grids and acceleration; “all NeRFs are slow MLPs” is misleading.
See the [original NeRF project](https://www.matthewtancik.com/nerf).

## Why several views matter

A large distant splat can look like a small nearby splat. A single image may
also trade opacity against color. Another viewpoint reveals parallax and changes
occlusion, constraining explanations that one camera cannot distinguish.
Even many views do not uniquely determine every Gaussian: hidden regions and
redundant primitives remain ambiguous.

Your first prediction: if you move a centered Gaussian twice as far from a
pinhole camera without changing its physical scale, its image standard deviation
approximately halves. Its image covariance approximately quarters. Derive that
in chapter 2, then verify it in `test_projection_rotation_and_depth_scaling`.

**Checkpoint:** explain why moving a 3D Gaussian affects every training view,
but moving a painted 2D blob affects only its own image. If that distinction is
clear, you understand why this is a 3D reconstruction problem.
